How it works

Each latitude band (36 bands; substellar bands for tidally locked worlds) solves an energy balance with full spectral radiative transfer. Thermal emission comes from a line-envelope model of H₂O, CO₂, CH₄ and SO₂ with the water continuum and collision-induced absorption, calibrated to Earth's clear-sky OLR (265 W/m²), CO₂ forcing (3.7 W/m² per doubling) and the 282 W/m² steam-atmosphere limit. Sunlight goes through a 9-layer two-stream scheme that uses your star's actual spectrum.

Slow processes run alongside: the carbonate–silicate cycle (weathering, volcanic outgassing, ocean carbon, carbonate breakdown on hot surfaces), ice sheets, a deep ocean, hydrogen escape (diffusion- or energy-limited) that strips water and leaves O₂ behind, bulk atmospheric erosion, CO₂ frost, and the star's own evolution.

Time is tied to the wall clock, not your frame rate. The solver is implicit and adaptive, so it automatically takes small steps through fast transitions (a runaway boiling an ocean off over thousands of years) and huge steps through quiet Myr. If your computer can't keep up, the achieved rate is shown honestly.

Try: push instellation past ~1.15 S⊕ for a moist greenhouse, or ~1.25 S⊕ for a full runaway. Drop it below ~0.94 S⊕ and the planet snowballs, then thaws once volcanic CO₂ builds up over a few Myr. Remove most of the water to make a desert world, tidally lock it to make an eyeball, or shrink it to Mars size.
